# PART 2: ARCHITECTURAL, LEGAL & OPERATIONAL DEEP-DIVE

## 1. Statutory & Ethical Framework: Why Maintenance is Mediatable While Dowry is Barred

### The Compoundable Civil Claim

The maintenance claim operates within a well-established statutory framework designed to facilitate amicable resolution. Under Family Courts Act 2023 Section 5, a wife and minor child possess statutory entitlement to reasonable maintenance. The Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 Section 9 reinforces the husband's obligation. Crucially, the Domestic Violence Act 2010 Section 16 vests jurisdiction to grant monetary compensation, living expenses, and child support.

These provisions create what is legally classified as a compoundable civil dispute. The subject matter concerns quantifiable financial obligations capable of negotiation without compromising public policy or enabling criminal conduct. The Code of Civil Procedure 1908 Sections 89A and 89B institutionalized mediation precisely for such matters, with settlements recorded in prescribed form becoming enforceable court decrees with no right of appeal barring fraud.

Section 21A of the Legal Aid Services Act 2000, as amended in 2026, empowers the District Legal Aid Officer to conduct pre-case mediation. Critically, Section 21A(4) provides that any settlement executed before the Legal Aid Officer carries the legal force and effect of a civil court decree under CPC Section 89A. This statutory architecture demonstrates legislative intent that family financial disputes should be resolved through structured mediation before formal litigation.

The ethical foundation for mediating maintenance is equally sound. UNODC standards on restorative justice recognize that financial disputes between parties with ongoing relationships benefit from consensual resolution processes that preserve dignity and enable future compliance. For Moyuri, mediation offers tangible advantages: no court appearance, no travel to Joypurhat, no adversarial confrontation, and the possibility of receiving regular financial support without prolonged litigation.

### The Non-Compoundable Criminal Offense

The dowry demand, by contrast, involves fundamentally different legal character. Under Dowry Prohibition Act 2018 Section 3, demanding dowry directly or indirectly constitutes a cognizable, non-bailable criminal offense punishable by up to five years imprisonment and fine. The specific conduct alleged, demanding a motorcycle backed by threats of second marriage and abandonment while simultaneously subjecting Moyuri to nighttime battery, falls squarely within this prohibition.

The Domestic Violence Act 2010 Section 3 further characterizes economic abuse, coercive dowry demands, and domestic battery as domestic violence. These offenses are classified as non-compoundable under Bangladesh criminal jurisprudence and Supreme Court doctrine. Non-compoundable offenses cannot be bargained away, settled, or compromised through mediation because doing so would condone criminal coercion, undermine public policy, and endanger the victim.

The ethical prohibition is absolute. A legal aid mediator cannot legally negotiate a discount on an illegal dowry demand. The mediator's statutory role under Section 21A does not extend to authorizing criminal conduct or permitting a perpetrator to purchase immunity from prosecution. Permitting mediation of the dowry demand would transform the legal aid system into an instrument of extortion, allowing Sohel to continue his coercive demands while enjoying protection from criminal liability.

The Supreme Court of Bangladesh has consistently held that domestic violence and dowry-related offenses cannot be mediated because the power asymmetry between perpetrator and victim prevents genuinely voluntary consent. A woman living in her husband's family home, surveilled by in-laws, whose national ID card is locked away, and who can speak for only twenty minutes per week cannot negotiate freely. The conditions of coercion vitiate any apparent consent.

## 2. Low-Bandwidth Asynchronous Caucus Design

### Why Video Conferencing Fails

The case presents a technological chasm that renders conventional video conferencing systems (Zoom, Microsoft Teams, WebRTC-based platforms) completely unviable. Moyuri's connectivity is limited to an unstable 2G cellular network that drops for hours. She possesses no internet access and has never opened a browser. Her device is an old button phone held by her mother-in-law and returned each evening for call log inspection.

Even if Moyuri could access a smartphone, the mandatory joint video session would place her in an impossible position. She has no private space within her in-laws' home, no ability to speak freely without being overheard, and no capacity to appear before a screen without triggering immediate suspicion. A joint video call would expose her to emotional manipulation, intimidation, and potentially retaliation once the session ended.

Sohel's circumstances present parallel obstacles. Though he possesses a smartphone in Gazipur, he works long industrial shifts at a rice mill with limited break windows. His discomfort with technology mirrors Moyuri's lack of digital literacy. Requiring him to navigate a complex app, manage bandwidth, and participate in a scheduled video hearing would likely result in non-appearance and system failure.

### The Tele-Caucus Solution

The asynchronous tele-caucus model resolves these constraints through deliberate design choices. Rather than requiring simultaneous participation, the mediator conducts separate audio sessions with each party at times and locations suited to their circumstances.

For Sohel, sessions are scheduled during factory break periods. He receives an IVR call at a predetermined time, allowing him to participate using his existing smartphone without navigating any application interface. The call requires no internet connectivity, no data package, and no technical literacy. He simply answers the phone and speaks.

For Moyuri, sessions are conducted through a secure audio connection established at the Union Digital Center or during her Friday safe window. A female paralegal attends in person to ensure privacy and provide reassurance. The mediator speaks with Moyuri alone, without Sohel or his family present, creating a genuinely confidential environment where she can articulate her needs without fear.

The shuttle diplomacy approach allows the mediator to transmit proposals between parties without direct confrontation. When Sohel offers a maintenance figure, the mediator conveys this to Moyuri and returns with her counter-proposal. This asynchronous exchange can occur over days or weeks, accommodating network drops, work schedules, and safe windows without requiring continuous connectivity.

The system architecture stores all communication records on the DLAS server, accessible to the DLAO at Joypurhat. Voice recordings are transcribed and tagged by case ID, creating an auditable record of the mediation process. The mediator's notes document the substance of each session without requiring either party to possess email, file storage, or document management capabilities.

The tele-caucus model is not merely a technical compromise but a substantive design choice that enhances mediation quality. Separate sessions reduce the risk of domestic bullying during negotiations, allow the mediator to explore settlement options privately with each party, and ensure that any agreement reflects genuine assent rather than coerced compliance.

## 3. Zero-Screen Accessibility & Safety Protocol

### Addressing Ripon's Blindness

Ripon's visual impairment presents a specific accessibility challenge. He cannot read screens, solve captchas, receive OTPs, or navigate visual interfaces. However, his reliance on voice and memory represents an opportunity rather than merely an obstacle.

The zero-screen voice gateway eliminates all visual barriers. Ripon dials 16699, the national legal aid helpline, and connects with an IVR voice agent. The agent guides him through the intake process verbally, asking questions in Bangla and recording his spoken responses. At no point does Ripon encounter a screen, form, PDF, or captcha.

His existing capabilities, dialing phone numbers, taking calls, and recording voice messages, are precisely the skills the system requires. The nephew who provided his smartphone can perform the initial app installation if necessary, but thereafter Ripon operates entirely through voice. The system stores his contact information and case ID, enabling him to call the helpline at any time to check case status or add information.

For notifications, Ripon receives automated voice calls in Bangla. The system employs text-to-speech technology to convert written case updates into audible messages. When the mediator needs to convey a settlement offer to Ripon, the system calls him and plays the recorded message. Ripon responds by speaking, and the system records and transcribes his reply.

The Union Digital Center serves as an additional accessibility touchpoint. When Ripon visits the Kalai UDC, the entrepreneur there can assist with voice capture and document scanning. Ripon's thumbprint, or his signature witnessed by UDC staff, validates his participation. The physical presence of a trusted local actor compensates for digital access limitations without requiring Ripon to acquire new technical skills.

### Protecting Moyuri's Safety

Moyuri's safety concerns are more acute. She is surveilled by her mother-in-law throughout the day. Her button phone is returned each evening for call log inspection. Any unscheduled message, call, or notification could expose her engagement with the legal aid system and trigger domestic retaliation.

The system addresses this through strict communication protocols. First, all outbound communications to Moyuri's phone are prohibited. She will never receive an SMS, IVR call, or missed call notification on the surveilled button phone. The system flags this constraint at intake and enforces it across all modules.

Second, the mother's phone number is registered as the verified safe contact. Communications intended for Moyuri are routed to her mother, who can convey messages in person without creating a digital trail. Alternatively, communications occur through the female paralegal at the Union Digital Center during Moyuri's visits.

Third, the Friday safe window is treated as a protected communication period. The system schedules all direct interactions with Moyuri during the twenty-minute window when Sohel is at the mosque and her mother-in-law is resting. Even within this window, the system uses the secure audio connection at the UDC or the mother's phone, never the button phone under surveillance.

Fourth, all case documentation is maintained exclusively on the DLAS server. No physical documents are sent to Moyuri's home address. No written communications are delivered through postal channels. The DLAO reviews digital records at Joypurhat, and any physical documentation required for the final decree is handled through the UDC with female staff presence.

The safety protocol extends to the mediation process itself. Before any joint or shuttle negotiation begins, the female legal aid officer conducts a confidential risk assessment. She explores whether Moyuri faces active battery, whether dowry demands include threats, and whether the family environment permits any exercise of free will. If any indicator of danger emerges, the system immediately transitions to the emergency judicial pathway described below.

## 4. Enforceability & MFS Accountability

### Converting Settlement to Court Decree

The mediation outcome achieves legal force through the statutory architecture of Section 21A. When the mediator finalizes settlement terms, the agreement is documented in the prescribed form and transmitted electronically to the DLAO at Joypurhat.

The DLAO reviews the settlement record, verifying that both parties consented voluntarily and that the terms comply with statutory requirements. The DLAO then applies a digital judicial signature, formalizing the agreement under Section 21A(3) and 21A(4) of the Legal Aid Services Act 2000.

This certification transforms the mediated settlement into an enforceable decree. Under Section 21A(4) and CPC Section 89A, the settlement carries the full evidentiary force and enforceability of a civil court or family court decree. If Sohel defaults on any payment or obligation, Moyuri can execute the decree without filing a fresh lawsuit or presenting evidence anew.

The digital decree is stored on the DLAS server with appropriate security protocols. The DLAO, the Family Court at Joypurhat, and the executing court all access the same authoritative record. No physical document needs to travel between jurisdictions. The digital signature authenticates the decree's validity, and the audit trail documents every step from intake through certification.

This architecture eliminates the traditional barriers to enforcement. Moyuri does not need to travel to Joypurhat. She does not need to hire a lawyer. She does not need to file papers at the court complex. The digital decree, once certified, is immediately executable.

### MFS Integration and Automated Accountability

The settlement's financial terms are integrated with Mobile Financial Services to create automatic accountability. The DLAS system generates a payment schedule based on the mediated agreement, recording the monthly maintenance amount, payment deadline, and recipient wallet details.

Sohel receives automated reminders through his preferred channel, IVR call or SMS to his smartphone, alerting him to upcoming payments. When he remits funds, he uses his DLAS case ID as the transaction reference. The MFS provider (bKash, Nagad, or Rocket) processes the transfer and credits Moyuri's mother's verified wallet.

Upon successful transfer, the DLAS system records the payment and sends a silent voice confirmation to Ripon. No SMS reaches Moyuri's surveilled button phone. The payment confirmation is audible only to Ripon, who can inform his mother and sister in person.

If Sohel fails to remit by the tenth of the month, the system generates an automated warning call. This call informs Sohel that his payment is overdue and that continued default will trigger enforcement proceedings. The warning provides a grace period before escalation.

If default exceeds thirty days, the system automatically files an Execution Petition under Family Courts Act 2023 Section 14. The petition is generated electronically, attaching the certified decree and the payment ledger showing the default. The executing court at Joypurhat can then issue orders attaching Sohel's wages from his Gazipur employer without requiring Moyuri to appear or file any additional documents.

This automated enforcement mechanism addresses the most significant weakness of traditional maintenance orders: the burden of repeated court appearances and fresh filings. By integrating the payment ledger with the decree, the system creates a self-executing accountability framework that operates without Moyuri's active involvement.
